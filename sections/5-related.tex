\section{Related Works}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{{\it Verification}}  Proving the correctness of distributed
protocols and models is a topic of significant recent
interest~\cite{KA+07,HH+15,SWT18}.  These approaches provide strong
correctness guarantees but require significant investment on the part
of the proof engineer (e.g., by defining suitable inductive
invariants) to complete a verification task~\cite{PM+16,YR+21,MG+19}.
Our focus in this work is to improve the effectiveness of
falsification techniques - validating the presence of bugs in a
distributed protocol design, rather than their absence.  The burden we
impose on test engineers, providing handler specifications as PAT{}s,
as well as a global safety/liveness property in LTL$_f$, is a
significantly lower hurdle than what is required to verify functional
correctness.

\paragraph{{\it Testing}}   There have also been
numerous efforts at improving the capabilities of testing frameworks
for distributed systems~\cite{Jepsen,BK+21,RK+19}.  For example,
Jepsen~\cite{Jepsen} uses randomization to explore schedules that may
reveal bugs when an application is deployed on a weakly-consistent
storage system. Similarly, MonkeyDB~\cite{RK+19} uses a demonic
scheduling mechanism to expose safety violations in SQL applications
that interact with a weakly-isolated storage backend.  In contrast,
\name\ is applicable to \emph{any} distributed application or protocol
that can be modeled as an open reactive system.  Our approach can also
be seen as a form of property-based testing (PBT)~\cite{CH00,HC+24}
applied to this setting.  Broadly related to our approach is
Mocket~\citet{WD+23}, a PBT-style testing framework that uses the
state space graph extracted from model-checking TLA+
specifications~\cite{Lam02} that enforce executions to follow specific
paths in the graph.  Unlike \name, Mocket requires manual
instrumentation of implementations to align actions defined in the
specification with the corresponding code in the implementation.  It
also relies on the TLC model-checker to produce the state space graph
that it explores.  On the other hand, \name\ leverages a compositional
refinement type system to drive synthesis, and requires no
instrumentation or \emph{a priori} enumeration of the state space to
synthesize effective bespoke controllers.

\paragraph{{\it Specifications}}
Type and effect systems that target \emph{temporal} properties on the
sequences of effects a program may \emph{produce} is a well-studied
subject. For example, Skalka and Smith\cite{SS+04} present a type and
effect system for reasoning about the shape of histories (i.e., finite
traces) of events embedded in a program.  Koskinen and
Terauchi\cite{KT+14} present a type and effect system that
additionally supports verification properties of infinite traces,
specified as B\"{u}chi automata.  More recently,
\cite{Temporal-Verification} consider how to support richer control
flow structures, e.g., delimited continuations, in such an effect
system.  Closest to our work, is the proposal of Zhe {\emph et
  al}~\cite{ZYDJ24} on \emph{Hoare Automata Types} (HAT), an
integration of symbolic finite automata within a refinement type
system, suitable for reasoning about stateful sequential programs
structured as a functional core interacting with opaque effectful
libraries.  Our development of \PAT{}s, while sharing some obvious
commonality with HATs, extends them with prophency automata to enable
their use in a distributed setting, where traces represent both
constraints on the history of consumed messages as well as
requirements of future messages that have yet to be handled.  

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}

This paper examines a property-guided testing framework for open
reactive distributed system models.  Our key innovation is the use of
prophecy automata types (\PAT{}s) that enable the specification of
message handlers in terms of a trace history and future.  A
component-based synthesis procedure leverages \PAT{}s to output
bespoke test controllers specialized to generating execution traces
that violate a given safety or liveness property.  Experimental
results on a wide range of benchmarks, including real-world models
used in production, show that \name\ is significantly more effective
in uncovering design bugs than the existing state-of-the-art.
